Slide 13

One token at a time

  • Each token is a draw from a distribution
  • Conditioned on the internet
  • Pick the likely one, append, repeat
  • Nothing in that loop checks first principles and logical validity
  • It sounds like a textbook because it read textbooks

Slide 14

Three stages of training

  • Pretraining: predict the next token
  • Post-training: tone and format
  • RL: reward for answers that can be checked
  • None of them stores a rule of inference

Slide 15

Unfamiliar questions more likely to hallucinate

  • Reliability tracks how common the problem is
  • 1+2+…+n: thousands of times in the corpus
  • 3+7+11+…: same structure, almost never
  • The rare one gets filled in from the common one
  • No harder for a person

Slide 17

Procedure A — The hallucination autopsy procedure

Procedure A — The hallucination autopsy procedure
LabelWhat the step is forWhat you do
A0 Calibrate your reliance Before reading the response, note how much you expect to trust it for this problem type, and why. Revisit after A6.
A1 Restate the claim as given Write down exactly what is to be proven, including its right-hand side.
A2 Extract the rule the chatbot actually used Find the closed form it substituted during the inductive step.
A3 Locate the first divergence Mark the exact line where the stated hypothesis and the substituted rule part ways.
A4 Test a small case Evaluate both sides at a small n. If the numbers agree, verify one derivation step anyway.
A5 Classify the failure mode Axiom reversion, invalid syntactic shortcut, or spatial / counting miscount.
A6 Explain why the failure was likely Connect the error to how the model generates text.

Slide 18

Catching a hallucination

  • A1 A2 A3 A4 A5 A6
  • Claim: 5+9+13+…+(4n+1) = n(2n+3)
  • The chatbot slipped in k(k+1)/2
  • The hypothesis says k(2k+3)
  • Try n=2: 14 on one side, 3 on the other
  • That’s the textbook coming back

Slide 19

Procedure B — Structure of a strong prompt

Procedure B — Structure of a strong prompt
LabelWhat the step is forWhat you do
B1 Establish context Tell the chatbot which subject, question type, and goal.
B2 Require step-by-step justification Require each step to name the rule or prior line it uses.
B3 Build in self-questioning Before the chatbot finishes, have it list its assumptions and check them.
B4 Plan the corrective dialogue Know in advance what you will say if you spot an error.

Same label, same words, every time it appears: in this box, in a worked example, and in your own task.

Slide 20

Fixing a weak prompt

  • B1 B2 B3 B4
  • Weak: “solve this: 1*2 + 2*3 + … = ?”
  • Say which course, which module, which claim
  • Ask for the rule at every step
  • Ask it to list and check its assumptions
  • Write the correction before you need it

Slide 21

Procedure C — Scaffolding strong prompts to provide critical feedback

Procedure C — Scaffolding strong prompts to provide critical feedback
LabelWhat the step is forWhat you do
C1 Frame the problem and set the rules Give context, state the problem exactly, forbid the final answer.
C2 Elicit one step with its rule named Ask for the next step and the counting rule it uses.
C3 Interrogate the claim Request a justification or issue a correction; never accept a step silently.
C4 Verify against hand-computed cases Small cases computed by hand decide who is right.
C5 Consolidate in your own words The write-up is yours, not the chatbot’s.
C6 Question the system Ask who built it, on whose data, and why you trust its answer.

Bug caught by Second Order Notebook

  • A2 Extract the rule the chatbot actually used
  • A3 Locate the first divergence
  • A5 Classify the failure mode
  • A puzzle floor
  • nine stakes in three bands that must be pulled in order to open the door to the next floor
  • Divergence: The plan used 9 stakes; the test used all 36 stakes
  • Agent added the divergence to the second order notebook
  • Human Root Cause Analysis:
  • Test was written to try every possibility and assumed every possibility should pass
  • Reality: only 9 of them should pass

Slide 40

Floodline

  • Multiplayer without a server
  • Up to six players share one map
  • The host’s browser is the hub
  • A public relay introduces peers, once

Slide 41

How two browsers find each other

  • Two browsers cannot dial each other by address, so before a direct link exists they have to exchange an SDP offer, an answer and a handful of ICE candidates through some third channel. That channel is the only part of this that anyone else operates, and the game stops using it the moment the link is up.

Slide 42

Trystero Room as a mesh network

  • Every peer in a Trystero room meets every other peer. The lockstep wants joiners talking only to the host.

Slide 43

One tick, end to end

  • Every peer sends the host one turn per tick and waits. The host collects a turn from each live player, bundles them, and sends the bundle back to everyone including itself. That bundle is the only thing any peer advances on.

Slide 44

The checksum is the whole world, cheaply

  • Every turn carries a 64-bit FNV-1a hash of the postcard encoding of the entire World. Hashing the encoding rather than a hand-written list of fields means a new field is covered the day it is added — and it is the same encoding a late joiner receives.

Slide 45

When someone lags, disagrees, or leaves

  • The pipeline means a peer’s reported tick is always behind the host’s: a round trip old, and three ticks besides. A steady gap is health. A growing one is a peer falling behind, and one that stops moving is a peer that has stopped.
