Sam Gilson

A student is stuck on a proof. It’s late, the assignment is due, and they open a chatbot.

What happens next is what I study.

Sometimes a student reads the answer, sees that it’s wrong, and argues with the machine until it isn’t. Sometimes a student reads the answer and copies it down. Both hand in the same assignment, so a grade will not tell them apart. The chat logs will, and separating help-seeking that supports learning from help-seeking that replaces it gives an instructor something to design the next assignment around.

So I rewrite discrete mathematics questions until a chatbot gets them wrong, hand them to a class along with a chatbot, and read the conversations.

I’m a PhD student in the Game2Learn lab at NC State, where Dr. Tiffany Barnes is my advisor. I teach CSC 151, Java Programming, at Central Carolina Community College. At NC State I have taught CSC 226, Discrete Mathematics, and CSC 216, Software Development Fundamentals. Two of the studies needed software that did not exist, so I wrote the Perturbation Workbench and PDF Redactor; both run in a browser and both are on GitHub.

My studies

Three first-author studies. Each one started because I wanted to know something I could not look up.

  • Adversarial Assignment Perturbation: Effects on Help-Seeking Behaviors in Student Generative AI Chatbot Use Artificial Intelligence in Education (AIED 2026), LNAI 16585, 2026 · Conference paper

    A chatbot gets a proof wrong. One student notices, pushes back, and ends up at the right answer. Another writes the wrong one down. This is a case study of the 8 students in an undergraduate discrete mathematics course who chose to use a chatbot on an assignment I had rewritten until the chatbot could not solve it. Both patterns are in the chat logs, and within that small sample they tracked the grades on the perturbed assignment.

    The full record, with BibTeX · doi:10.1007/978-3-032-29770-9_48
  • Designing AI-Resistant Assignments via Iterative Perturbation to Promote Interactive Learning Proceedings of the 57th ACM Technical Symposium on Computer Science Education V.2 (SIGCSE TS 2026), 2026 · Poster

    Writing an assignment a chatbot fails is harder than it sounds, and it is easy to write one that defeats the students as well. This poster is about the making: hand the question to a chatbot, and when the chatbot answers it well, change the question and hand it over again, until the chatbot stops getting it right. What counts as resistant is a threshold fixed before any of the rewriting starts, so the target cannot move to meet the result.

    The full record, with BibTeX · doi:10.1145/3770761.3777058
  • How Do Undergraduates Interested in Computer Science Use Online, On-Demand CS Coursework? Proceedings of the Twelfth ACM Conference on Learning @ Scale (L@S '25), 2025 · Conference paper

    A student signs up for a free online course, works through a few modules, and the trial runs out. I surveyed 51 undergraduates taking or interested in computer science and interviewed four of them, asking what sent them to that coursework and what made them stop. Most had gone looking for interview practice and something to put on a resume. What they turned to when the free access ended was ChatGPT.

    The full record, with BibTeX · doi:10.1145/3698205.3733938
