Long Time Comin'

Game

Long Time Comin' is a third-person shooter that runs in a browser, in the bullet-hell style of Returnal and Saros: enemies fire large orbs in rings, spirals and flowers, and you dodge through them while shooting back. In the story you committed a crime in 2081 that the court calls the Stilling, and were sentenced to go back and stop yourself. The court's Bailiff sent you fifty years too early, so you land in 2031 and have to cross the half century on foot.

Each run starts in the first zone, the Waiting City, a graph of arenas. One main path runs from the crater where you land to the Court of Hours and its boss, the Bailiff of Hours. Side paths of one or two harder fights leave the main path and rejoin it, and each ends in a reward shown on the map before you choose: a higher level of one of the three guns, more health, more speed, another jump (up to four), or one of three that last the run: shots that pierce, a dash that clears the orbs round you, or a parry that heals. Before the boss, the Antechamber offers half your health back or one more reward, and you take one. Every run rolls its own graph.

Beating the Bailiff does not end the run. In an interlude the years pass: you use what you know of the coming decades to build a company, and in 2046 you pay for an expedition north to fetch the Observatory's key from the ice before your older self can. The second zone, the White Waste, is built the same way, with its own seven enemies and its own boss, the Witness. Once you have beaten the Bailiff, a run can also start on the ice.

Beating the Witness does not end the run either. It tells you that you will do something so terrible that time itself stops, and then it cannot finish the sentence. You go home and spend ten years and a fortune on laboratories in your house, looking for a way to keep time from breaking, until in 2056 an organisation of men in grey suits who all have the same face raids the house and kills your scientists. The third zone, the Glass House, is that house: a great hall with twin staircases, a dining room, a library of tall shelves, a laboratory whose floor has broken through into the basement, a long gallery and a ballroom, all with ceilings high enough that the camera has room. Its seven enemies are people. They walk the floors and stairs, hide where you cannot see them and step out to fire, wait for a partner so that two of them fire at the same moment from either side of you, go round behind you, lob mines over cover, and close walls of orbs on either side of you so that you have to run for the ends. A mini-boss in the ballroom keeps calling more of them. The boss, the Executor, is a tank that drives in through the conservatory's glass wall. Only the glowing weak points on its back take damage, each phase grows more cannons out of it, and its fourth phase, covered in eyes and turning wheels of cannons, cannot be hurt at all: you have to survive it.

The fourth zone, the Ascent, is the evening of 14 March 2081 itself, the clock always at 21:14. You climb Mount Meridian toward the Observatory, and every arena stands higher up the mountain than the one before it, with the cloud and the city's lights further below each time. The mountain's own creatures are turning on its terraces: harts whose antlers end in clock hands, rooks with eyes along their wings, toads, a man-eating plant that lunges to bite, a stream risen as a body of water, toadstools and bears. The ritual that will stop time is putting clockwork in them, and what is coming is putting eyes in them, more the higher you climb. They come ten or more at a time, with enemies from the zones below and assassins in your own coat, sent by your older self. Partway up, a horned owl the size of a cart sits on dead snags high round a terrace, firing down, and swoops through where you stand to strike with its talons. At the summit, in a circle of standing stones round the Long Door, is the Defendant: you, older, with every ability you have and one more. They stop time, hold you still while they walk round you laying their shots in the air, and then let time go. Beating them gives you the same power, and then the game shows what the stopped evening was holding back. Two mini-bosses were added to the earlier zones as well: a walking mech on the ice and a smaller tank in the house.

The fifth zone is inside the Idiot God, the thing your ritual let in. Its spaces are impossible, after the trick-art levels of Ultrakill's latest layer and games like Manifold Garden, Antichamber and Hyperbolica. Fall off any floor and you come down on it again from above, with the arena repeating above and below you. Gold doorways lead to other doorways, for you and for the orbs, and one stair of four flights climbs for ever. In the distance, space curls up into a bowl and breathes. Its seven enemies each look as if they came from a different game: an eight-bit invader that marches in steps, a tesseract drawn in the glowing lines of a vector display, an engraved figure in one-bit dither that moves only when you are not looking at it, a PlayStation-era hound that turns on the spot and then charges, rubber-hose cartoons that all bounce on one beat, a photographic negative of you that walks the path you walked three seconds ago, and a golden fractal that splits into smaller copies of itself when it dies. The Idiot God has a health bar for each of its three phases. When the last one empties it does not die. Instead you take out a trumpet: its dancers pipe a verse, and you play it back in time on five keys, as on the ocarina in Ocarina of Time, while it keeps firing on the beat and you keep dodging. Four verses put it to sleep inside a bubble of stopped time, and that is the game's ending.

When you die, the court reads the next of the ten counts of your indictment and adds years to your sentence, and you return to the Holding, where the Clemency the run earned buys upgrades. The 32 upgrades are laid out as a tree in four branches. Four of them add mechanics: a grappling hook that hauls you to what it hits and keeps your speed when you let go, a parry that turns gold orbs back at whoever fired them, the Injunction, a wall in front of you for half a second that breaks blue orbs (two later upgrades let it break more colours), and a ground pound that drops you straight down, sends a shockwave along the floor and bounces you back up past where you started. The house also adds a fourth gun, the Ripsaw, which throws saw blades that stick in what they hit and grind while you hold the trigger. Every gun has a second fire on the right mouse button: the Carbine fires a three-round burst, the Scattergun a slug, the Launcher a fan of bombs, and the Ripsaw shatters every blade it has lodged. The mountain adds a fifth gun, the Big Iron, a six-shot hand cannon whose rounds are heavy and slow and which loads one shell at a time; its second fire fans the hammer and empties the cylinder. In every zone you can also grab an enemy that is badly hurt and throw it into the others. Once you have beaten the game, the Holding also offers a boss rush, each zone's boss one after another, and a test save button loads a record with everything unlocked.

The arenas have height. In the city there are bridges and walkways to climb or run under, towers, broken walls to hide behind, and in the boss's court, stands that its ground waves cannot reach. On the ice there are updraft vents that throw you up, a crevasse you can fall into, an icebreaker frozen in the ice to run through and climb, a catwalk round a field of radio masts, and an ice cave with a roof. The enemies on the ice place their orbs in shapes and hold them before they fly: a ring hangs in the air, then its outer ring flies and its inner ring follows at twice the speed; a cage forms round you and closes; a grid of orbs waits and then flies at where you are heading. When the camera ends up behind a wall or inside something, the shader cuts away the scenery in front of you so it never hides you. An in-game Codex lists every control, every level of every gun, every enemy and the orbs each attack fires, every upgrade and the rules, all written by the core from the numbers the game runs on.

Every rule runs in a Rust core in fixed-point integer maths, compiled to WebAssembly, and the page draws what the core returns. Each enemy pattern is data: how many orbs a volley holds, the shape they are placed in, how long each is held, how far each repeat turns, and how fast each orb curls or changes speed. The arenas are built from a seed, and every shape is outlined in ink to match the menu art, which is drawn in TikZ.

Playing it

W, A, S and D move and the mouse aims. Hold the left button to fire, and press the right button for the gun's second fire. Space jumps, and again in the air if you have the jumps; Shift dashes, and orbs pass through you while you dash. E takes a gun from the floor, F fires the hook, Q parries, R raises the Injunction and C in the air is the ground pound, once bought. G grabs and throws, and T stops time once you have earned it. When the trumpet comes out, the left button, Q, E, R and F play its five notes. Tab opens the map and Escape pauses. Your record is kept in the browser you played in.
